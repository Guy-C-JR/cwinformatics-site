Solutions/By problem

Organized around the problems teams actually bring us.

Each capability starts with a situation, names who it tends to matter to, and lists the questions it is built to help answer. If one of these sounds familiar, that is a good place to start a conversation.

We can’t see what drives our AI costs.

Relevant to: engineering leaders, platform teams, and the finance partners who review the invoice.

Usage visibility

A clear account of how AI requests consume resources, broken down by workflow and request shape, including how much of each request is repeated material. The intended outcome is a shared, specific understanding of where processing goes, so that decisions rest on observation rather than guesswork.

  • Which workflows generate the most processing, and is that proportionate to their value?
  • How much of a typical request is context the model has already seen?
  • Where would a closer investigation be worthwhile, and where would it be a distraction?

We suspect our requests carry more than they need.

Relevant to: teams running assistants, document review, or retrieval-based workflows with long or reused context.

Optimization assessment

An evaluation of whether the information in a request can be handled more efficiently while preserving the context the task depends on. The intended outcome is a short list of candidates worth pursuing, and an equally clear list of requests that should be left alone.

  • Which parts of a request does the task actually depend on?
  • Is repeated material being processed as if it were new every time?
  • Which requests are too fragile or too sensitive to change at all?

We want to optimize, but not blindly.

Relevant to: teams with quality requirements, regulated content, or customer-facing AI features.

Controlled application

Policies that decide where optimization is applied, where it is only observed, and where it is switched off entirely. The intended outcome is change that is scoped, deliberate, and reversible, with the people who own a workflow deciding what happens to it.

  • Which workflows should optimization apply to, and under what conditions?
  • How do we keep certain workflows untouched by design?
  • What happens when we need to step back to the previous behavior?

We need to show it works before we roll it out further.

Relevant to: leaders who must justify a change, and engineers who have to live with it afterwards.

Measured evaluation

A baseline of the workflow as it stands today, and a comparison against it that accounts for efficiency, task quality, and the effect of caching. The intended outcome is a decision supported by measurement from your own workload, rather than a general claim.

  • What did the workflow look like before anything changed?
  • Did task quality hold on the cases that matter?
  • How much of any difference is caching, and how much is the change itself?

Where to start

Visibility first. Everything else follows from it.

Most engagements begin with understanding usage, because the other capabilities depend on it. An assessment needs something to assess. A policy needs something to govern. An evaluation needs a baseline. If you are unsure which problem you have, that is a normal place to be, and a conversation about your workflow is the fastest way to find out.
